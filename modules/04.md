# 04 — Continuity before complexity

status: draft
objective: NAV-01

## Observable goal

Distinguish line continuity from knowledge of exit direction.

## Opening scene

Fictional classroom case. A permanent mainline starts inside the cave rather than in open water. Mira can see it from the entrance and considers swimming directly to it without deploying a primary reel.

## Exploration

A. Imagine the same route with no visibility.

B. Ask what a primary and secondary tie-off each contribute.

## Branch consequences

A: A visible gap is still a gap. Once visibility disappears the team lacks a continuous tactile route across that space.

B: The primary tie-off establishes the open-water reference; the secondary tie-off secures the route and limits consequences of disturbance. Neither is a substitute for a suitable installation.

## Mandatory explanation

Maintain a continuous guideline to the proven open-water exit. A primary reel connects the entrance to the established line where needed. Avoid line traps, loose line, abrasion and locations where the line could lead a diver into an impassable space. Team members need to know the installation and exit direction, not simply follow a leader's fins.

Continuity and direction are separate questions. A line can continue into the wrong branch. The team must understand its route, references and decision points. Physical line installation and evaluation require instructor observation; this text does not specify attachment geometry or knot sequences.

## Worked application

In the schematic, Open water E connects by the team's primary line to tie-off S, then to mainline M. Removing E–S breaks continuity even if M remains visible. Adding E–S restores a connected route, but deciding between branches farther along still requires orientation.

## Misconception and feedback

Confusion: a visible mainline is equivalent to a connected mainline. Correction: visibility is not a continuous physical reference.
